Beyond the Shinkansen Stop: Niigata

Most people ride the Joetsu Shinkansen through Niigata thinking about ski season. Here's why you should get off at the rice fields, the sake counter, and the island nobody mentions.
July 30, 2026

Ask a foreign traveler what they know about Niigata and you’ll usually get one of two answers: “Isn’t that where the good skiing is?” or a blank stare. Both answers miss the point. Niigata City sits on the Sea of Japan coast, about two hours from Tokyo Station on the Joetsu Shinkansen, and it produces more sake than almost anywhere else in the country — plus rice so good it has its own cult following, an island with more temples per capita than Kyoto, and a coastline most people never think to look at because they’re too busy staring at Mount Fuji out the other side of the train.

I went for what was supposed to be one night. I stayed three, mostly because I underestimated how long it takes to properly work through a sake tasting flight, and partly because the ferry schedule to Sado Island made zero sense to me on day one and I needed a full extra day to figure it out.

First, the basics#

Niigata Station is the terminus — you can’t accidentally ride past it the way you might blink and miss Shin-Aomori. From Tokyo it’s about 2 hours on the Hayabusa or Toki shinkansen, roughly ¥10,000-11,000 one way if you’re not using a Japan Rail Pass (the JR East Nagano/Niigata pass covers this route and is worth doing the math on if you’re also hitting Nagano).

The station itself has been renovated recently, and the whole west side now connects directly into a shopping and dining complex called CoCoLo. This matters because CoCoLo houses Ponshukan, which brings me to the next section.

The sake counter that will humble you#

Ponshukan is a tasting facility inside the station where, for a ¥1,000 coin, you get five wooden tasting cups (sakazuki) and a machine dispensing samples from around 100 different sake breweries across the prefecture. You pick a coin size — the vending machine works exactly like an arcade token dispenser — and you go station to station tasting whatever catches your eye.

I want to be honest about what happened to me here. I told myself I’d try eight or nine and move on to lunch. I tried closer to twenty, because everything tasted different from everything else and I got competitive with myself about identifying tasting notes I clearly didn’t have the vocabulary for. Lunch happened around 4pm. If you’re going, pace yourself, eat something first, and maybe don’t plan anything requiring fine motor skills for the following two hours.

There’s also a salt bath for your feet made from the local hot spring water, built into the same facility, presumably for people in exactly my situation.

Sado Island: the trip nobody tells you to take#

This is the part of the itinerary that made people back home ask “wait, there’s an island?” Sado sits about 32 nautical miles off the Niigata coast, and jetfoils from Niigata Port make the crossing in about an hour (¥6,500-7,500 one way; the slower car ferry takes 2.5 hours and costs less).

Once you’re there, you need a rental car — public transit exists but it’s sparse, and the island is bigger than people expect (about the size of Tokyo’s 23 wards, minus the density). A day rental runs roughly ¥6,000-8,000.

Two things worth the drive: the Sado Kinzan gold mine, a former Edo-period mining complex you can walk through with mannequins recreating the labor conditions in unsettling but fascinating detail (entry around ¥1,000), and a ride in a tarai bune — a traditional tub boat, literally a washtub-shaped vessel that local women once used to harvest seaweed and shellfish among the rocks, still operated as short rides for visitors near Ogi (about ¥700 for ten minutes). It sounds like a tourist gimmick until you’re the one being paddled around a cove by someone whose grandmother did this for a living, and the boat is somehow completely stable despite looking like it shouldn’t be.

If you have a full day on Sado, that’s realistically what fits — the gold mine in the morning, lunch in Ogi or Aikawa, the tub boat in the afternoon, and the last jetfoil back before dark. Don’t try to add more; the drive times between sights are longer than the map suggests.

The rice, and why it matters here#

Niigata’s Koshihikari rice is the prefecture’s other headline export, and once you’ve eaten it plain — just rice, maybe with a pickle on the side — at a proper restaurant here, supermarket rice back home starts tasting like an afterthought. Look for restaurants advertising “shinmai” (new-harvest rice, available roughly September through November) if you’re visiting in autumn; it’s noticeably different from rice that’s been sitting in storage.

For a casual, honest meal, the Furumachi district has a cluster of small izakaya where a full dinner with a few small plates and a couple of sake pairings runs about ¥3,500-5,000 per person.

A 2-night model plan#

Night 1 — arrive in Niigata City. Check in, walk to Furumachi for dinner (¥4,000). Early evening at Ponshukan if you didn’t already blow your tasting budget on arrival.

Day 2 — Sado Island, full day. Early jetfoil out (¥7,000 round trip), rental car (¥7,000), gold mine (¥1,000), lunch (¥1,500), tarai bune ride (¥700). Last boat back, dinner near the station.

Day 3 — Niigata City, half day before departing. Bandai Bridge and riverside walk, a final sake shop or two, lunch, shinkansen back to Tokyo.

Rough total for two nights excluding shinkansen fares and accommodation: ¥25,000-30,000 for two people, mostly eaten up by the Sado day trip.

Where to Stay#

Niigata City is compact enough that staying anywhere within walking distance of the station works fine, and it also puts you closest to the jetfoil terminal for the Sado crossing. Find hotels and guesthouses near Niigata Station on Booking.com — mid-range business hotels in the ¥7,000-10,000/night range are clustered a short walk from the station’s west exit.
